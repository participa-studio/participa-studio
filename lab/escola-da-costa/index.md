challenge: 'For more than a year I had been looking for enterprises that put design to work for social and regenerative purposes. Research and conversations in my network led me to the <a href="https://institut.design">Institut Supérieur de Design de Saint-Malo</a>, on the French coast, and I went to see it in person with its co-founder, Thiago Máximo. It is a cooperative, owned with its town and partners, and students learn on real assignments from local businesses and government. It showed me that design education can take a completely different form. I asked plainly: what would it take to have a school like that on the Brazilian coast? Ubatuba became one candidate location, through my friend Felipe Schmidt Fonseca, who lives there; I also looked at Rio Grande do Norte, in connection with a university group. Ubatuba sits between the Atlantic Forest and the ocean, with no local higher education; young people leave and rarely return. And caring about the ocean is not the same as understanding it: in a 2022 survey of 18 to 24 year-olds in 35 countries, 75% were worried about ocean health, yet 47% believed it was still healthy.'
approach: 'The approach was exploratory: conversations, a course and reading, each testing the concept from a different side.</p><p><strong>Form.</strong> In Saint-Malo I spent the day with Thiago and had dinner with a Fab Lab founder from Saint-Malo who now lives in Asia. We talked informally about how such a social enterprise could be organised. My first version leaned towards a structured organisation with its own location. Felipe pointed out that a fixed, established place has its own problems, and leaned towards a decentralised platform closer to a Fab Lab. Neither model convinced me as a future social enterprise.</p><p><strong>People.</strong> In IDEO U’s <a href="https://www.ideou.com/products/designing-for-change">Designing for Change</a> course, completed in November 2025, I mapped the groups the school would serve and prepared interview questions for specialists and community leaders.</p><p><strong>Presentation.</strong> An application to <a href="https://www.designscience.studio">Design Science Studio</a>, BFI (<a href="https://www.bfi.org">bfi.org</a>), a programme for artists, designers and systems thinkers, gave me the reason to research the concept properly. I documented it, produced videos, presented it to the group and wrote a proposal for the Artist Salon. The programme leaned towards personal and performative arts, and I was looking for someone to talk with about building a social enterprise. It matured the concept, but it was not the forum to develop it.</p><p><strong>Grounding.</strong> One path I explored was <a href="https://univ-cotedazur.eu/msc/marres">MARRES</a>, the Blue Managers track of the MSc in Ocean Science, Conservation and Innovation at Université Côte d’Azur, a one-year programme for careers in blue sustainable development, with Escola da Costa as my professional project. Before a formal application, the programme asks for a call with its office to check that the project is sound. In November 2025, we discussed my background and the project. I did not enrol in the end, but preparing for it taught me more about what it would take to make Escola da Costa a real enterprise. Reading in marine social science, social design and anthropology anchored the concept in life on the coast: an ongoing conversation between a community, the ocean in front of it and the materials at hand.'
outcome: 'None of it was built: no cooperative, no site, no funding. The more the concept grew, the less tangible it became, and I could not formalise it. What carried over is the frame: education as the point, not the product. <a href="/lab/refloat/">ReFloat</a> started as taking end-of-life boats apart for recycling, which many small enterprises already do. It took shape when Escola da Costa’s frame came in: the boat recycling workshop becomes a school, and the boat becomes a vector into a community conversation about the ocean, its nature and its role in the community. In that sense, ReFloat builds on where Escola da Costa stopped. Its thinking also shaped the principles behind Participa Studio.'
