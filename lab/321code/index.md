challenge: 'In 2013, coding was starting to be called a new literacy, but there were few playful ways for young children to learn it. The question for the weekend was whether an app could teach coding in a fun way at each age, and whether parents would pay for it. From all first-night pitches, I chose to join the one aimed at young children''s learning, building on what I had learned with LEGO three years earlier.'
approach: 'Startup Weekend follows a fixed rhythm. On Friday night, anyone can pitch an idea in a minute; everyone votes, and teams form around the strongest ones. Saturday is for quick and dirty research and building: who the customer is, what already exists, and what is the smallest thing worth showing. Sunday ends with a five-minute pitch to a jury, judged on business model, customer validation and execution. We mapped the apps already on the market. In mid-2013, Scratch was the reference for children but did not run on tablets, and a few new iPad apps, such as Daisy the Dinosaur, Hopscotch and Kodable, were just appearing. We defined parents as the paying audience, with tech-savvy parents as the early adopters, and native apps for phones and tablets as the channel. The marketing plan moved in three steps: awareness, credibility and trial. I worked on the concept with the team, designed the interaction across four age sets (1–6, 7–12 and so on), and shaped the story for the final pitch.'
outcome: 'We pitched 321CODE on Sunday, 7 July 2013, and received very good feedback from the jury and the audience. I keep it here because of how it was made. The pressure of 54 hours pushed me towards a strongly geometric design: simple shapes for the animals and the interface, quick to draw and easy for a young child to read. We tested it the fast way, sending the screens to real parents and collecting their impressions in short conversations, and the design landed well. That way of working, a small team under time pressure testing with real people within hours, later became common in hackathons and design sprints. For me, it was a first hands-on look at designing for learning, years before Participa Studio.'
